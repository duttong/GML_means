Part 1 · The problem

A miracle chemical with a hidden cost

Meet CFC-11

CCl3F
trichlorofluoromethane
1 carbon · 1 fluorine · 3 chlorine

CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons) were invented in 1928 as “miracle” chemicals: non-toxic, non-flammable and cheap. By the 1970s they were everywhere.

Fridges
Air conditioners
Spray cans
Foam

The catch: CFCs are so stable that nothing in the lower air breaks them down. CFC-11 lasts about 52 years, plenty of time to drift up to the ozone layer.

Earth’s sunscreen

Ozone (O3) absorbs most of the Sun’s UV-B rays, which cause sunburn, skin cancer and cataracts, and harm crops and sea life.

The ozone hole. In 1985, scientists found ozone over Antarctica plunging each spring, as frigid clouds there supercharge chlorine’s attack. The “hole” still returns every September to November.

Why it took so long to notice

In 1974, chemists Mario Molina and F. Sherwood Rowland warned that chlorine from CFCs could destroy stratospheric ozone. Many people doubted them, because CFCs seemed harmless at ground level.

The Antarctic ozone hole, reported in 1985, proved the danger was real. Two years later, nations signed the Montreal Protocol to phase out CFCs. In 2009 it became the first United Nations treaty ratified by every country.

Molina, Rowland and Paul Crutzen shared the 1995 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for their ozone research.

How one chlorine atom wrecks ozone

Step through the chain reaction. Watch what happens to the chlorine atom at the end.

  1. 1Drift up. Over years, CFCs rise into the stratosphere, home of the ozone layer.
  2. 2Break. UV light frees a chlorine atom.
    CCl3F + UV → Cl + CCl2F
  3. 3Attack. Chlorine tears ozone apart.
    Cl + O3 → ClO + O2
  4. 4Repeat. The chlorine is set free to strike again.
    ClO + O → Cl + O2

    Part 3 · Science in action

    Watching the sky, catching a cheater

    Case file: the CFC-11 mystery

    After 2012, CFC-11’s fall suddenly slowed by half. Clue #2: northern air pulled further ahead of southern air. Someone in the north was making CFC-11 again, despite the ban.

    NOAA scientists sounded the alarm in 2018. Studies traced much of it to eastern China, and emissions soon dropped.

    Your turn

    Read the graph like a scientist

    Use the charts above to answer each question, then check your thinking.

    1. In what year did CFC-11 reach its highest level? About how many years after the Montreal Protocol was that?

    CFC-11 peaked in 1994 at about 268 ppt (1993 was almost identical). That is about 7 years after the 1987 Montreal Protocol. Factories took years to stop, and CFCs already inside fridges, foam and air conditioners kept leaking out.

    2. Using the yearly-change chart, compare how fast CFC-11 fell in 2003–2012 with 2014–2018.

    From 2003 to 2012 it fell about 2 ppt per year. From 2014 to 2018 it fell only about 1 ppt per year. Something was adding new CFC-11 to the air. See the Case file above.

    3. Switch to CFC-12. It was banned at the same time as CFC-11, so why has it dropped less from its peak?

    CFC-12 lasts about 100 years in the air, roughly twice as long as CFC-11. Even with almost no new emissions, it disappears slowly. Compare the Report card: the longer a gas lasts, the smaller its drop.

    4. Switch to HCFC-22. What changed around 2021, and why?

    HCFC-22 rose for decades because it replaced CFCs in air conditioners. It peaked in 2021 and is now falling. HCFCs damage ozone less than CFCs, but they still contain chlorine, so the Montreal Protocol is phasing them out too.

    5. Switch the chart to “By latitude band.” Why was northern air so much higher than southern air in the 1980s, and why is the gap so small now?

    Most CFCs were used and released in the Northern Hemisphere, and it takes about a year for air to mix across the equator. While emissions were large, the north stayed ahead. With emissions now small, the hemispheres have nearly evened out.

    The road to recovery

    From warning to healing

    1. 1928CFCs are invented for refrigerators.
    2. 1974Molina and Rowland warn that CFCs could destroy ozone.
    3. 1985Scientists report the Antarctic ozone hole.
    4. 1987The Montreal Protocol is signed.
    5. 1994CFC-11 in the air peaks at 268 ppt (NOAA data).
    6. 1995Nobel Prize in Chemistry for ozone research.
    7. 1996Developed countries stop making CFCs.
    8. 2009Every country has ratified the Montreal Protocol.
    9. 2010CFC production ends worldwide.
    10. 2016The Kigali Amendment adds heat-trapping HFCs.
    11. 2018NOAA reports new, illegal CFC-11 emissions.
    12. 2025CFC-11 falls 2.9 ppt in one year, its fastest drop on record.
